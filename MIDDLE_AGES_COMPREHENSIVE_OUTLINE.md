# Middle Ages Track: 30-Lesson Comprehensive Outline
## Medieval Christianity A.D. 590–1517
### Based on Schaff Volumes IV, V, VI & Church 101

---

## **PART I: FOUNDATIONS & EARLY MEDIEVAL SYNTHESIS (Lessons 1–5)**

**1. What Was Medieval Christianity? The Three Pillars**
- Definition: transition from Roman to Germanic civilization; theater of Europe
- The three institutional forms that dominated: Papacy, Monasticism, Scholasticism
- Christianity as the binding force of fragmented Europe
- Key theme: Christianity saved the best of the old, shaped the new
- *Sources: Schaff IV intro; Church 101 overviews*

**2. The Nations of Medieval Christendom: Kelts, Teutons, Slavs**
- Kelt: eloquent, unstable, loyal; struggled with self-governance ("shook empires but founded none")
- Teuton: disciplined, orderly, "predestinated for Christianity"; the advancing races
- Slav: isolated from Western current; later became Orthodox; Russia as Eastern Christendom
- Greek: maintained independence; tension with Latin West
- *Sources: Schaff IV's ethnographic analysis; Church 101*

**3. Pope Gregory the Great (A.D. 590–604): Founder of Medieval Papacy**
- The last of the Fathers, first of the Popes
- Organized missionary movements on imperial scale
- Built papal administrative infrastructure
- The crisis: "form of godliness without power"—nominal Christianity emerges
- Gregory's vision as foundation for medieval papal authority
- *Sources: Schaff IV extensive Gregory chapters; Church 101 7th century*

**4. Saint Benedict and Early Monasticism (6th Century)**
- Benedict's life: from hermit to founder
- The Rule of St. Benedict: prayer, work, community governance
- Monte Cassino and monastic expansion
- Monasteries as spiritual, educational, and economic centers
- *Sources: Schaff IV on monasticism; Church 101 6th century on Benedict*

**5. Early Medieval Learning, Culture & the Preservation of Civilization**
- Monastic scriptoria: preserving classical texts
- Eastern learning vs. Western ignorance
- Justinian's codification of Roman law
- Literary revival under Charlemagne and Alcuin
- Key figures: Bede, Alcuin, John Scotus Erigena, Gerbert
- *Sources: Schaff IV "State of Learning"; Church 101 8th century*

---

## **PART II: MISSIONARY MOVEMENTS & NATIONAL CONVERSIONS (Lessons 6–10)**

The heart of early medieval Christianity: how entire peoples moved from paganism to faith. Schaff devotes enormous attention to this arc.

**6. The Conversion of Ireland & the British Isles (5th–7th Centuries)**
- Saint Patrick: life, capture, escape, training, return as missionary bishop
- Unique Irish Christianity: monastic, independent, scholarly
- Saint Columba and Iona: Irish monastic expansion northward
- Augustine's mission to Anglo-Saxon England (A.D. 595)
- Synod of Whitby (664): Rome triumphs over Celtic practices
- The Culdees and gradual Roman conformity
- *Sources: Schaff IV Irish/Celtic/Anglo-Saxon chapters; Church 101 6th-7th centuries*

**7. Continental Missions: Clovis, Columbanus & Saint Boniface**
- Clovis and the Franks (A.D. 496): alliance of church and state begun
- Irish missionaries on the continent: Columbanus and monastic expansion
- Saint Boniface (A.D. 672–754): Apostle of Germany
- Conversion of Frisia, Hesse, Bavaria; organization of dioceses
- Boniface's martyrdom and legacy
- *Sources: Schaff IV chapters on Franks, Columbanus, Boniface; Church 101 8th century*

**8. Charlemagne, Alcuin & the Conversion of the Saxons (A.D. 768–814)**
- Charlemagne's dual approach: missionary preaching + military conquest
- The Saxon Wars: forced conversions and resistance
- Educational revival: Alcuin and the palace school
- The Heliand: Gospel harmony in Saxon vernacular
- Founding of the Holy Roman Empire (A.D. 800)
- *Sources: Schaff IV Charlemagne chapters; Church 101 8th-9th centuries*

**9. Northern Missions: St. Ansgar & Scandinavian Conversion**
- Ansgar (A.D. 801–865): "Apostle of the North"
- Mission to Denmark and Sweden: slow, difficult progress
- Norse resistance: heathenism deeply rooted in culture
- Later conversions in Denmark (10th c.), Sweden, Norway, Iceland
- *Sources: Schaff IV Scandinavian chapters; Church 101 10th century on Viking conversion*

**10. Eastern Missions: Cyril, Methodius & Russia's Conversion**
- Cyril and Methodius: Apostles of the Slavs (9th century)
- Creation of the Glagolitic alphabet for Slavic Scripture translation
- Christianization of Moravia, Bohemia, Poland
- Vladimir the Great and Kiev (late 10th c.): Choice of Orthodox Christianity
- Birth of the Russian Orthodox Church; massive Slavic baptisms
- Islam's rise and its relationship to Christian mission
- *Sources: Schaff IV Slavic chapters; Church 101 7th, 9th, 10th centuries*

## **PART III: INSTITUTIONAL DEVELOPMENT & CHURCH-STATE TENSIONS (Lessons 11–14)**

**11. The Early Medieval Papacy: From Gregory to the Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals (590–858)**
- Papacy after Gregory: consolidation of power
- Donation of Pepin and the Papal States (8th century)
- Papal alliance with Frankish kings
- Pope Nicholas I (858–867): claim to supreme authority
- The Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals: forged documents legitimizing papal supremacy
- Foundation for later medieval papal claims
- *Sources: Schaff IV papal chapters; Church 101 7th-9th centuries*

**12. The Degradation & Recovery of the Papacy (10th–11th Centuries)**
- The "Dark Ages" tenth century: papal corruption, temporal interference
- Otto the Great's intervention: emperor controls pope
- Moral and spiritual crisis in the papacy
- The beginning of papal reform: Leo IX and church renewal
- *Sources: Schaff IV 10th century sections; Church 101 10th century on "Dark Ages"; Schaff V on reform popes*

**13. The East-West Schism (1054) & The Conflict of Traditions**
- Centuries of theological, liturgical, jurisdictional tensions
- Photius in the 9th century: first major resistance to papal claims
- Patriarch Cerularius vs. papal legate (1054): final break
- Greek East vs. Latin West: language, worship, authority
- Consequences: medieval Christendom permanently divided
- *Sources: Schaff IV East-West chapter; Church 101 9th century (Photius foreshadowing); Schaff V setup*

**14. Church & State in England: William, Anselm & Thomas Becket**
- William the Conqueror and Lanfranc: Norman Conquest reshapes church
- William Rufus and Archbishop Anselm: conflict over lay investiture
- Henry I and Anselm: reconciliation and compromise
- Thomas Becket and Henry II: martyrdom of the archbishop (1170)
- Effects of Becket's murder: papal victory in England
- *Sources: Schaff V English conflict chapters; Church 101 12th century context*

## **PART IV: THE INVESTITURE CONTROVERSY & THE CRUSADES (Lessons 15–18)**

**15. Gregory VII and Henry IV: The Investiture Controversy (1049–1122)**
- Pope Gregory VII (Hildebrand): reformer and theocrat
- The "Gregorian Theocracy": papal supremacy over emperors
- The War over Investiture: who appoints bishops—pope or king?
- Henry IV's challenge to papal authority
- Canossa (1077): Henry's humiliation and reconciliation
- *Sources: Schaff V Gregory VII chapters; Church 101 11th-12th century context*

**16. The Concordat of Worms (1122) & Papacy after Gregory**
- Settlement of investiture dispute: compromise between pope and emperor
- Innocent III (1198–1216): apex of medieval papal power
- Innocent III's theory: papal supremacy in all Christendom
- Innocent's conflicts with emperors and kings
- The Fourth Lateran Council (1215): Innocent's defining achievement
- *Sources: Schaff V post-Gregory chapters; Innocent III sections*

**17. The Crusades: Origins, Movements & Effects (1095–13th c.)**
- Character and causes: religious zeal + political/economic motives
- First Crusade and capture of Jerusalem (1099)
- The Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem (1099–1187)
- Subsequent crusades: Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth, Later Crusades
- The Fourth Crusade: sack of Constantinople (1204)—devastating to East-West relations
- St. Louis and the last crusades
- Effects: enormous casualties, failed objectives, permanent enmity, internal Christian damage
- *Sources: Schaff V extensive Crusade chapters; Church 101 12th century*

**18. Military Orders & Crusading Ideology**
- The Templars, Hospitallers, Teutonic Knights
- Function: protection of pilgrims, military campaigns, banking
- Wealth and corruption of the orders
- The crusading mentality: holy war as spiritual duty
- Critique: theological problems with crusading ideology
- *Sources: Schaff V military orders chapter*

## **PART V: MONASTIC & MENDICANT RENEWAL (Lessons 19–21)**

**19. The Cistercians & Saint Bernard of Clairvaux (12th Century)**
- Revival of monasticism after degradation of 10th century
- The Cistercians: reform of Benedictine Rule, return to simplicity
- Saint Bernard (1090–1153): mystic, preacher, reformer
- Bernard's influence on church and spirituality
- Cistercian architecture, agriculture, expansion
- *Sources: Schaff V Cistercian chapters; Church 101 12th century*

**20. The Franciscans & Saint Francis of Assisi (13th Century)**
- Francis (1182–1226): soldier turned radical reformer
- Response to Matthew 10: poverty, simplicity, preaching
- The mendicant ideal: friars without property
- Franciscan expansion and influence
- Tension with papal authority and church hierarchy
- *Sources: Schaff V extensive Francis chapters; Church 101 13th century on Francis*

**21. The Dominicans & Mendicant Orders (13th Century)**
- Saint Dominic and the Order of Preachers (founded 1215)
- Focus on preaching, pastoral ministry, theological education
- The mendicant model: four major orders (Franciscans, Dominicans, Carmelites, Augustinians)
- Mendicants vs. traditional monasticism
- Role in universities and theological development
- *Sources: Schaff V Dominican chapters; Church 101 13th century*

## **PART VI: UNIVERSITIES & SCHOLASTICISM (Lessons 22–24)**

**22. Rise of Universities: Bologna, Paris, Oxford, Cambridge (12th–13th c.)**
- Why universities emerged: need for trained clergy, intellectual revival, urban growth
- University of Bologna (founded ~1088): law and medicine focus
- University of Paris (founded ~1150): theology and arts center
- Oxford and Cambridge: English alternatives
- The role of guilds and masters
- Cathedral schools vs. universities
- *Sources: Schaff V university chapters; Schaff VI on Renaissance universities*

**23. Early Scholasticism: Anselm, Abelard & Peter Lombard (11th–12th c.)**
- Anselm of Canterbury: faith seeking understanding; ontological argument
- Peter Abelard: dialectical method; controversy over atonement
- Abelard's teaching method: "sic et non" (yes and no)
- Peter Lombard and The Sentences: compilation of patristic theology
- The scholastic method takes shape: reason in service of faith
- *Sources: Schaff V scholasticism chapters; Church 101 12th century on Abelard*

**24. High Scholasticism: Albertus Magnus & Thomas Aquinas (13th c.)**
- Albertus Magnus (1193–1280): master of sciences and theology
- Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274): "the dumb ox" becomes "Doctor of the Church"
- Aquinas's major works: Summa Contra Gentiles and Summa Theologiae
- Philosophy as handmaiden of theology
- Aquinas on Scripture, authority, tradition
- Bonaventura, Duns Scotus, Roger Bacon: competing scholastic visions
- *Sources: Schaff V Aquinas chapters; Church 101 13th century on Aquinas*

---

## **PART VII: DOCTRINAL & THEOLOGICAL DEVELOPMENT (Lessons 25–26)**

**25. The Sacramental System & Eucharistic Theology**
- Development of seven sacraments in medieval theology
- Transubstantiation doctrine: bread and wine become flesh and blood
- Paschasius Radbertus vs. Ratramnus: early eucharistic controversy
- The Berengar Controversy (11th c.): realism in eucharistic theology
- Lanfranc's triumph: transubstantiation becomes orthodox doctrine
- Eucharistic practice and popular superstition
- *Sources: Schaff IV and V eucharistic controversy chapters*

**26. Predestination, Free Will & Grace in Medieval Theology**
- The tension: divine foreknowledge vs. human free will
- Gottschalk and Hincmar (9th c.): early medieval debate
- Semi-Augustinianism: medieval compromise position
- Thomas Aquinas and the synthesis of reason and revelation
- The filioque controversy: theological and political dimensions
- *Sources: Schaff IV predestination chapter; Schaff V theological synthesis*

---

## **PART VIII: LATE MEDIEVAL CRISIS & SPIRITUAL RENEWAL (Lessons 27–28)**

**27. Papal Decline: Boniface VIII, Avignon & the Schism (1294–1417)**
- Pope Boniface VIII (1294–1303): last claim to absolute papal power
- Conflict with Philip the Fair of France
- Transfer of papacy to Avignon (1309–1377): 70-year exile
- Financial corruption of Avignon popes
- The Great Schism (1378–1417): rival popes in Rome and Avignon
- Reform councils: Constance (1414–1418), efforts to heal the schism
- *Sources: Schaff VI papal decline chapters; Church 101 context*

**28. Mysticism & the Devotio Moderna (14th–15th c.)**
- Medieval mysticism: direct experience of God beyond sacrament and hierarchy
- Meister Eckhart (1260–1327): German mystical theology
- John Tauler (1300–1361): practical mysticism
- Thomas à Kempis and The Imitation of Christ: devotional classic
- Gerrit de Groote and the Brothers of the Common Life: lay piety movement
- Mysticism as critique of institutional corruption
- *Sources: Schaff VI mysticism chapters; Church 101 implicit critique of institutional church*

---

## **PART IX: HERESY, INQUISITION & POPULAR RELIGION (Lessons 29–30)**

**29. Medieval Heresies & Their Suppression (12th–15th c.)**
- The Cathari (Albigenses): dualistic heresy; crusade against them
- The Waldenses: evangelical poverty movement; persecution for centuries
- Peter Waldo's "poor men": early medieval reform impulse
- The Lollards (England): John Wycliffe's followers
- The Hussites (Bohemia): reform movement; religious wars
- Causes of heresy: reaction to papal corruption, desire for apostolic simplicity, theological deviation
- *Sources: Schaff V heresy chapter; Schaff VI heretical movements*

**30. The Inquisition, Witchcraft & the Final Medieval Crisis (1393–1517)**
- Origins of the Inquisition: medieval heresy control mechanism
- The Spanish Inquisition: persecution of Jews and Muslims
- Procedure and penalties: torture, execution, confiscation
- Witchcraft persecution: rise in 15th–16th centuries
- The Renaissance humanist movement: classical learning revival
- The sale of indulgences: papal fundraising and spiritual abuse
- The final medieval crisis: Corruption, moral decay, institutional rot
- Leo X and the papacy on the eve of Reformation
- *Sources: Schaff VI Inquisition, witchcraft, Renaissance, indulgences chapters; Church 101 implicit endpoint*

## **DESIGN PHILOSOPHY**

- **Chronological + Thematic**: moves through time while organizing by institutional/doctrinal themes
- **All three sources integrated**: Schaff's detail + Church 101's clarity
- **Three pillars structure**: Papacy (institutional), Monasticism (spiritual), Scholasticism (intellectual)
- **Emphasis on conversion narratives** (early medieval) and institutional/theological development (high and late medieval)
- **Both East and West**: not Rome-centric; includes Orthodox, Slavic, and Greek developments
- **Concrete figures**: Gregory, Patrick, Columba, Boniface, Ansgar, Bernard, Francis, Aquinas, Eckhart, etc.
- **Progression of themes**: from missionary zeal → institutional power → theological sophistication → spiritual crisis → reform impulses
